01 Briefing

The corpus,
read end to end.

One hundred thousand Amazon reviews went through an eleven-stage NLP pipeline — every review scored, split by aspect and grouped into topics, and five thousand of them condensed and embedded. This is what came back.

How this works Corpus analytics · results of the offline pipeline
  1. Dataset. 100,000 Amazon reviews: 50,000 positive and 50,000 negative, sampled from the public Amazon Review Polarity dataset (pipeline/01).
  2. Offline NLP, run once. VADER labelled every review (pipeline/06), 28 product aspects were detected (07), NMF found 10 topics (08), 5,000 reviews were summarized (09) and embedded as vectors (10). The results were saved as CSV files.
  3. This page only counts. The server reads those CSVs and totals them. No model runs when this page opens.
  4. Charts. The six charts at the bottom were drawn ahead of time from the same CSVs by app/plates.py (matplotlib). Regenerate them with python -m app.plates.
Human labels vs VADER
Human labels come from star ratings: 1–2★ negative, 4–5★ positive, 3★ left out. So there is no human “neutral”. VADER is a lexicon-based model that can also answer neutral.
Model agreement
The share of reviews where VADER’s label equals the human label, i.e. VADER’s accuracy on this data. Most misses are complaints VADER read as positive, which is why the Verdict page adds product-review rules.

      02 Sentiment

      Hand it a review.
      Get the reasoning.

      A hybrid engine: VADER polarity fused with a product-review rule set of 122 phrases and 45 keywords. Every verdict shows its working — the exact language that moved the score, marked in the reviewer's own text.

      How this works Hybrid lexicon-based sentiment analysis · VADER + product-review rules
      1. Rule lexicon. Find the 122 phrases and 45 keywords of app/lexicon.py in the text. Rule score = 0.40 × positive phrases + 0.08 × positive keywords − 0.50 × negative phrases − 0.10 × negative keywords.
      2. VADER. A 7,500-word dictionary of rated words plus rules for negation (“not good”), intensifiers (“very”), “!!!”, CAPITALS and “but”. It outputs a compound score from −1 to +1.
      3. Combine. If only negative (or only positive) phrases were found, they decide the label. Otherwise final = 0.70 × compound + 0.30 × rule score; ≥ +0.05 is Positive, ≤ −0.05 is Negative, anything between is Neutral.
      4. Hard rules. “very slow”, “don’t buy”, “waste of money” and their variants always force Negative.
      Why a hybrid?
      On the 100k reviews, VADER alone labelled 24,180 of the 50,000 human-negative reviews positive. VADER scores complaints like “stopped working” only weakly (−0.23), so praise elsewhere outvotes them: “The screen is great… but it stopped working” gets +0.38 from VADER. The rule lexicon gives such phrases a fixed heavy weight, and agreement with the human labels rises from 70.45% to 76.27% (pipeline/12).
      Reading the output
      The highlighted words are the evidence. The numbered steps under “How the verdict was reached” show the exact arithmetic for this review.

      03 Facets

      What exactly
      are they judging?

      Reviews rarely praise or damn a whole product. They praise the battery and damn the screen. Twenty-eight product aspects, each detected by keyword and given its own polarity split.

      How this works Aspect-based sentiment analysis · aspect lexicon + VADER
      1. Aspect lexicon. 28 hand-picked aspect words: price, quality, battery, shipping, book, movie, music and more.
      2. Detect. A review mentions an aspect when that word appears as a whole word (regex \b…\b, so “price” does not match “priceless”). One review can mention several aspects.
      3. Score. The review’s VADER label (positive / neutral / negative) is attached to every aspect it mentions.
      4. Aggregate. Count the labels per aspect → aspect_sentiment_summary.csv.
      Reading the output
      Each bar shows the share of positive (green), neutral (grey) and negative (red) mentions. The figures are the mention count and net sentiment = (positive − negative) ÷ total, from −100% to +100%.
      Limitation
      The label belongs to the whole review, not to the sentence that names the aspect. “Great battery, awful screen” gives both aspects the same label.
      Code
      pipeline/07_aspect_sentiment.py (offline) → GET /api/aspects → app/server.py · aspect_table()

      04 Themes

      Ten conversations
      running at once.

      Unsupervised topic modelling (TF-IDF + NMF) over the full corpus. Pick a topic to see its keywords and read what it actually sounds like.

      How this works Topic modelling · TF-IDF + Non-negative Matrix Factorization
      1. Clean. Lowercase each review and strip URLs, digits and punctuation.
      2. TF-IDF. Turn each review into weights for 10,000 words and word pairs. A term weighs more when it is frequent in that review but rare across all reviews.
      3. NMF. Factorise the 100,000 × 10,000 matrix into reviews × 10 topics times 10 topics × terms, with no negative numbers allowed, so each review is an additive mix of topics.
      4. Keywords. A topic’s keywords are its 10 highest-weighted terms.
      5. Assign. Each review goes to its strongest topic (argmax).
      Unsupervised
      Nobody told the model what the topics are. It found groups of words that tend to occur together. NMF only outputs topic numbers; the short names were written by hand from each topic’s keywords.
      Reading the output
      The polarity bar shows how VADER labelled the reviews in that topic. The sample reviews are a fixed sample from the first 500 reviews whose strongest topic is this one, so they stay the same between visits.
      Code
      pipeline/08_topic_extraction.py (offline) → GET /api/topics → app/server.py · topic_list()

      05 Search

      Search by meaning,
      not by word.

      Describe the sentiment you're hunting for. The corpus is ranked against your description in embedding space — matching reviews need not share a single word with your query. Then pick any review and find the ones saying the same thing.

      How this works Semantic search & similarity · sentence embeddings + cosine similarity
      1. Embeddings, made offline. Each of the 5,000 corpus reviews was converted by the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 Transformer into a vector of 384 numbers that captures its meaning (pipeline/10).
      2. Search. Your query goes through the same model, so it lands in the same 384-dimensional space.
      3. Find similar. Instead of a typed query, use the stored vector of review #i (row i of review_embeddings.csv). No model is needed.
      4. Compare. Cosine similarity between that vector and all 5,000 review vectors, computed in one matrix multiplication.
      5. Rank. Return the highest-scoring reviews, best first. When finding similar reviews, the review itself (similarity 1.0) is skipped.
      Reading the score
      Similarity is the cosine of the angle between two vectors: near 1 means the same meaning, near 0 means unrelated. A review can score high without sharing a single word with the query.
      Fallback
      Without sentence-transformers installed, search runs in lexical mode instead: cosine similarity of IDF-weighted word counts. The left rail shows which mode is live. “Find similar” always works, because every vector is already stored.
      Review index
      The row number (0–4,999) in amazon_reviews_summarized.csv, the 5,000-review retrieval corpus.

      06 Summary

      Long review in.
      The gist out.

      Extractive summarisation by TextRank: sentences are ranked by how central they are to the review's own similarity graph. Nothing is invented — only the load-bearing sentences survive.

      How this works Extractive summarization · TextRank (PageRank on sentences)
      1. Split the review into sentences.
      2. Tokenize each sentence into words.
      3. Build a graph. Every sentence is a node. Two sentences are linked more strongly the more words they share.
      4. PageRank. Score the nodes, repeating until the scores stop changing (damping 0.85). A sentence scores high when it is similar to many other high-scoring sentences, i.e. it is central to the review.
      5. Select. Keep the top N sentences and print them in their original order.
      Extractive, not abstractive
      Sentences are copied, never rewritten, so the summary cannot say anything the reviewer did not.
      Two versions
      The live engine is the sumy library’s TextRank (it keeps stopwords; link = shared words ÷ (log|A| + log|B|)). Without sumy, a built-in TextRank runs instead: it drops stopwords and does 40 fixed rounds. Its maths is written out in app/summarize.py.
      Reading the output
      Compression = share of words removed. Reviews under 25 words, or with no more sentences than requested, are returned unchanged.
      Code
      POST /api/summarize → app/summarize.py · summarize() (sumy’s TextRank, with a built-in TextRank as fallback)
